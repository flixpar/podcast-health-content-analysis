Health talk across 132,000 podcast episodes: a fast lexical scan

What a 3,000-term keyword pass over 1.48 billion transcribed words can already say about where health information lives in the corpus, which contested narratives circulate, how they are framed and sold, and what that implies for the LLM labeling run.

1. Summary

  • Health talk is everywhere but thin. 88% of episodes contain at least one sentence on a health topic, yet only 9% of episodes are more than 5% health by sentence count. The median episode mentions health in passing; a small set of shows discuss it wall to wall.
  • Most health talk happens outside health shows. The 50 Health & Fitness chart shows carry 14% of the words but 40% of health sentences. The other 60% comes from comedy, politics, business and true crime shows, led by The Joe Rogan Experience, which alone accounts for 7% of all health sentences.
  • Topics split cleanly by show type. Vaccines, COVID, gender medicine, public-health policy and the health-care system are political-show topics: half or more of their sentences come from news and politics shows. Gut health, hormones, supplements, peptides, longevity and "functional medicine" are almost entirely wellness-show topics.
  • Contested narratives concentrate in wellness shows, not conspiracy shows. Per million sentences, health and wellness shows carry 3.6 times the narrative rate of news shows and roughly 100 times that of sports shows. The highest-rate shows are functional-medicine and biohacking programmes, where narratives like leaky gut, MTHFR and methylene blue are stated as routine fact. In the graded sample, 55% of relevant narrative sentences endorse the claim, 21% report it, 11% rebut it and 7% are sponsor reads; leaky gut was endorsed in 19 of 20 sentences, while vaccines-and-autism was endorsed in 5, reported in 9 and rebutted in 6.
  • The news-driven narratives move fast and broadly. The Tylenol-in-pregnancy story reached 35 shows; it surfaced in business-news shows on 9 September 2025, two weeks before the White House announcement, then spread across political shows within days. The May 2026 hantavirus cruise-ship outbreak produced the single sharpest burst in the corpus, 33 times its baseline.
  • Framing differs by genre in a measurable way. Political shows wrap health topics in industry and institutional distrust at three times the rate of wellness shows once bare company names are excluded (six times before). Wellness shows instead lean on personal experience and mechanistic language, and use absolute-certainty markers more often than any other genre. Correction and debunking language is rare everywhere and rarest in wellness shows.
  • Wellness narratives are legitimised by mechanism, not evidence. In a close reading of 200 narrative sentences from wellness shows, a third recite a biochemical pathway as settled fact, a quarter tie the claim to a product or the host's own protocol, and under one in ten names a checkable source. Vaccine talk splits the same way: political shows narrate the institutional fight, wellness shows discuss ingredients, injury and the individual decision (section 11).
  • Sponsorship is a health-claim channel. Seven in ten episodes contain a sponsor-read marker. One in four GLP-1 sentences and one in seven supplement sentences sit within four sentences of an ad marker. Section 10 quantifies which products and claims travel this way.
  • The LLM pipeline runs against the hosted DeepSeek API, and yield tracks the scan. A $4.48 pilot of the existing labeling pipeline on DeepSeek V4 Flash accepted 107 windows from 24 scan-selected episodes and extracted 176 atomic claims. Wellness episodes yielded about five claims per window, political and general episodes about one every two windows, so a production pass aimed at the health-chart shows buys most of the claim signal for a fraction of the corpus. The pilot also found a bug that stops the merge step on any current run (section 13).
  • Precision is label-dependent and now measured. A blinded manual audit of about 2,700 matched sentences (section 12) puts topic labels at 85% precision (concrete health terms at 95 to 100%), but the idiom-based framing labels near zero outside a health context, and only half of topic hits are substantive discussion rather than passing mention or ad copy. The report flags the weak labels rather than hiding them.

2. Context and approach

The project's main instrument is the LLM labeling pipeline in analysis/topic_labeling.py: 84 taxonomy labels, atomic claim extraction, expressed certainty, product mentions and a separate evidence-verification step. It is thorough and slow. A full pass with a reasoning model costs on the order of weeks of GPU time, so results will arrive gradually.

This report asks what can be learned right now with methods that run over the whole corpus in minutes. The answer is more than expected, provided the questions are chosen to suit the method. Keyword matching cannot judge whether a claim is true, or whether a speaker endorses it. It can count, locate and time things, and at 1.48 billion words, counting is informative: which shows carry which topics, how narratives distribute across the panel, when they burst, how they co-occur with framing and sponsorship language, and where the LLM effort should be pointed.

The design borrows the pipeline's taxonomy directly. Each of the 49 topic, 27 frame and 7 evidence labels in topics.md was given a list of high-precision terms and phrases. Three things were added that the pipeline treats differently: a set of 80 named contested narratives written as within-sentence patterns (for instance vaccin* … autis*), a list of about 100 sponsor products common on US podcasts, and marker lists for sponsor reads, distrust, correction and expressed certainty. Everything is matched at the sentence level, so co-occurrence within a sentence is the unit of framing analysis. Methods are in section 15; the manual precision audit is in section 12.

How to read the numbers. A "health sentence" is a sentence that matches at least one topic term other than the catch-all other_health_topic. Rates are per 1,000 or per million sentences so shows and genres of different sizes compare. Counts are of matched sentences unless stated as episodes or shows. A narrative hit means the pattern occurred, not that the speaker believes it; section 12 reports how often the sample was endorsed, reported, rebutted or read as an advertisement.

3. The corpus

The database holds 226 podcasts from four charts (Apple US top 100 at two dates, Spotify US top 100, Apple US Health & Fitness top 50); 220 of them have transcripts. Transcription is now effectively complete: 132,412 of 132,541 episodes, 134,662 hours, 1.48 billion words. Nine in ten transcripts are ASR output from one model (Qwen3-ASR-1.7B, ten-minute chunks); the rest are publisher transcripts pulled from RSS, which are the only ones with speaker labels (4% of transcripts).

Data quality notes relevant to this analysis

  • Only 71 transcripts have under 200 words. Words-per-minute outliers (1.4% of transcripts) are concentrated in sleep and meditation shows and do not affect the health analysis.
  • 1,738 episode rows are duplicate titles within a show (issue C2 in docs/corpus-issues.md); they inflate episode counts for a handful of shows by a percent or two and were not removed here.
  • ASR spells brands unpredictably. The lexicon includes spoken variants ("AG one", "B P C one fifty seven"), but at least one major sponsor (LMNT, transcribed as "element") is unrecoverable by keyword and its counts are a floor.
  • Segment timestamps in ASR transcripts are ten-minute chunk boundaries, so position within episode is measured by sentence index, not seconds.

4. Where health talk happens

Across all 136 million sentences, 2.17 million (1.6%) match a health topic. The audit in section 12 finds that about half of those are substantive discussion; the rest are passing mentions, jokes, biography or ad reads, so a fair estimate of sustained health discussion is closer to 0.8% of sentences. The distribution over episodes is heavily skewed: most episodes have a few such sentences, and a small tail is saturated with them.

Six in ten health sentences come from shows that are not on the Health & Fitness chart. The 50 chart shows contribute 13.6% of words and 40.3% of health sentences (55 per 1,000 sentences against 10.7 elsewhere). If the project's exposure question is "what health information does the average podcast listener hear", the general-audience shows matter at least as much as the health shows, and their health talk is qualitatively different: shorter, more topical, more political.

5. Which topics, and who owns them

Mental health is the most widespread topic by far: 46% of all episodes contain a mental-health sentence, driven by everyday use of "therapy", "anxiety" and "depression" across every genre. Fitness, nicotine, food, pregnancy and substance use follow. The heavily contested topics, vaccines and COVID, are large in sentence count but concentrated in fewer episodes.

Which shows carry which topic

For each topic, the share of its sentences that come from Health & Fitness chart shows and from news and politics shows tells you where the conversation actually happens. The two groups barely overlap.

6. Change over time

Two different clocks apply. The back catalogue covers 2006 to 2025 with a panel that grows every year, so long-run trends are only interpretable as rates within the shows that existed. The live window from 13 October 2025 observes the full panel at a steady 2,300 episodes a month and supports burst detection and diffusion analysis.

The hantavirus burst is the Andes-virus outbreak on the cruise ship MV Hondius, reported to WHO on 2 May 2026; it reached 59 shows and was carried mostly by news and comedy programmes rather than health shows, a pattern typical of outbreak news. The April 2026 rise in autism discourse and the October 2025 rise in public-health policy talk track the federal autism and vaccine-schedule announcements of that period. Wellness narratives such as leaky gut and methylene blue do not burst; they run at a steady rate, which is the signature of an established belief system rather than a news cycle.

7. Contested narratives

Eighty named narratives were written as tight within-sentence patterns. They range from classic vaccine misinformation to wellness claims that are contested rather than false, and from outbreak fear to sponsor-driven practices such as red-light therapy. Read the two together: a narrative with high precision and high endorsement (leaky gut, MTHFR, seed oils, beef tallow, adrenal fatigue) is a belief being taught; one with high precision and mostly report or rebut (Tylenol, vaccines-autism, hantavirus) is a news story being discussed; and a low-precision row (died suddenly, insurance denial, mRNA-changes-DNA) is a lexicon failure whose count should be discarded. Red-light therapy is the clearest sponsor-driven narrative: nearly half of its sampled sentences are verbatim device ads.

8. Belief bundles and scripted repetition

Two questions the counts alone do not answer: do narratives travel together, and how much of the narrative volume is one script replayed? Both are cheap to answer at the episode level.

Which narratives co-occur in the same episode

Pointwise mutual information (PMI) over episode co-occurrence measures how much more often two narratives appear in the same episode than chance would predict, on a log2 scale: a PMI of 5 means 32 times more often than chance. The strongest pairs sort into three bundles that match the show clusters seen in section 7.

Scripted repetition

Sponsor copy is inserted programmatically, so the same sentence recurs verbatim across episodes and shows. Counting sentences as independent observations therefore overstates how many speakers make a claim. After normalising case and punctuation, 13% of all narrative sentences are exact repeats of another sentence in the corpus; for the product-shaped narratives the share is far higher.

The practical rule: report narrative prevalence on distinct sentences, or better on distinct episodes, and treat any sentence that recurs verbatim across three or more episodes as an advertisement until shown otherwise. That single filter would reclassify most of the red-light, beef-tallow, hydrogen-water and NAD volume from "narrative" to "sponsor read".

Narrative breadth by show

Breadth separates two kinds of high-volume show. Culture Apothecary and Rogan touch almost every narrative in the list (70 and 69 of 80), which is the profile of a show that takes all comers; Dr. Jockers and the Thyroid Fixer have fewer narratives but far more sentences per narrative, the profile of a show with a fixed doctrine repeated across episodes.

9. Framing, evidence and certainty

Within health sentences, co-occurring markers give a rough profile of how each genre and show talks about health. Rates are per 1,000 health sentences. Restricting to sentences that already contain a health term is what makes these markers usable at all: judged on their own, the idiom-based frame labels are mostly political or true-crime talk (section 12). Even within health sentences the distrust column still counts plain news mentions of pharmaceutical companies, so treat it as "industry and institution framing", not as hostility.

  • Distrust framing is political. News and politics shows attach industry, institutional or conspiracy framing to 9 of every 1,000 health sentences, more than six times the wellness-show rate. Much of that is company names (Pfizer, Moderna) appearing in ordinary news copy: with bare company names removed from the lexicon (section 12, sensitivity table) the news rate falls to 3.6 per 1,000 and the wellness rate to 1.1, a gap of three times rather than six. The individual leaders (All-In, The Journal, Charlie Kirk, Morning Wire, Breaking Points) are unchanged.
  • Wellness shows argue from mechanism and experience, not from weak studies. They have the highest rates of personal-experience evidence (1.6 per 1,000, four times the news rate) and of absolute-certainty markers (23.8, the highest of any genre), and cite studies at about the corpus average. The apparent lead in weak-evidence signals (3.0 per 1,000) was an artefact: it was carried by the phrase "associated with", and once that phrase is removed the wellness rate (0.4) is indistinguishable from news (0.3). Wellness shows use "in mice" and "in a petri dish" no more than anyone else; their weakness is in what they assert, not in what they cite.
  • Correction language is rare everywhere. Under 1 in 1,000 health sentences carries a correction or debunking marker; the rate is highest in news (2.4) and education shows (2.1) and lowest in wellness (0.4). Within specific narratives the picture is starker: 4.6% of vaccine-autism sentences carry a correction marker, against 0.0 to 0.3% for leaky gut, methylene blue, red-light therapy, seed oils and raw milk.

12. Validation and limits

Independent spot checks

Because the headline numbers were produced by one script, a Sonnet agent was asked to recompute nine of them from the raw parquet and database without reading the analysis code, and to read the sentences behind the diffusion and burst claims. A Haiku agent separately re-graded 120 of the narrative audit sentences blind to the first grader's decisions, to estimate how reproducible the precision and stance figures are.

Result of the recomputation. All nine figures matched to the stated precision. The reading checks confirmed that the earliest Tylenol sentences are genuinely about the acetaminophen-autism announcement and that the May 2026 hantavirus sentences are all about the cruise-ship outbreak. One interpretation was sharpened: every one of 15 sampled "studies show" sentences inside ad windows was sponsor copy (a pet-food line on Rogan, "research shows dogs at a healthy weight live longer", and software reads on The Journal), so the 19% figure in section 10 measures borrowed-evidence language in advertising rather than science being cited near ads. That is the intended reading, but it also means the scientific_study_citation label is partly an ad detector.

Result of the blind re-grade. Agreement on whether a sentence is relevant was 78% (kappa 0.28); agreement on stance among sentences both graders accepted was 50% (kappa 0.26). The first grader was more willing to call a tangential mention relevant and to read plain factual statements as endorsement; the second required an explicit signal. Both readings are defensible, which is the point: the endorse/report boundary is genuinely ambiguous at sentence level without context, and the stance percentages in sections 1 and 7 should be read as indicative (roughly half of narrative mentions are assertions, a fifth to a third are reporting, about a tenth are rebuttals) rather than as measured to the percentage point. The LLM pipeline's discourse-role coding on full windows with context should do better than either single-sentence grader, and this inter-rater figure is the bar it needs to clear.

Sensitivity to lexicon pruning

The audits named roughly 75 terms as the main sources of false positives (bare "5G", "paid off", "died suddenly", "the Hateful Eight", bare pharma company names, "therapy", "smoking", "outbreak" and so on). A second lexicon with those removed was scanned over the full corpus and the analysis re-run. The table shows how the main figures move. Where a number barely changes, the finding does not depend on the noisy terms; where it drops sharply, the original count was mostly noise.

Three things stand out. The structural findings are robust: the share of health talk outside health-chart shows, the concentration in a few dozen shows, the wellness-versus-news narrative rate ratio and the vaccine and COVID topic counts move by two points or less. Several topic counts were substantially noise: EMF and technology exposure loses two thirds (phone-carrier "5G" reads), AI health information loses 90% (bare "ChatGPT"), performance-enhancing drugs loses three quarters (bare "steroids"), and mental health loses a quarter (bare "therapy"). Two framing results changed materially and the text above has been corrected: the news-show distrust rate and the wellness-show weak-evidence rate. The pruned lexicon (lexicon_v2.json) is the one to carry forward; its remaining known gap is recall on the terms that were dropped rather than qualified.

Structural limits of the method

  • No stance. A hit is a mention. Debunking shows and promoting shows both register. The audit gives the stance mix per narrative for the sample; only the LLM pass can give it per sentence.
  • No claims. The pipeline extracts atomic, checkable claims. The scan finds the vocabulary of claims. Many true health sentences ("I went to the gym") are not claims at all.
  • Sentence-level co-occurrence undercounts framing. A distrust frame two sentences before a claim is not captured. The ad-window analysis (section 10) uses a ±4 sentence window instead and finds three to five times more co-occurrence than the sentence-level figure.
  • Lexicon recall is unknown. Precision was measured; recall was not. Narratives phrased without their keywords, and topics discussed with vocabulary not in the list, are missed. Recall can only be estimated from the LLM labels on a uniform sample.
  • Panel composition. Long-run trends mix show entry with behaviour. Live-window results are clean; everything before October 2025 should be read as "among the shows then in the panel".
  • Speaker attribution is unavailable for 96% of transcripts, so host-versus-guest questions cannot be addressed until diarisation is added.

13. A first look with the LLM pipeline: DeepSeek V4 Flash pilot

To connect the scan to the pipeline it is meant to serve, a small budget-capped run of topic_labeling.py was pointed at the hosted DeepSeek API (spending guard set to US$4.50 of a US$5 allowance) over about two dozen live-window episodes chosen with the scan as a sampling frame: narrative-dense wellness episodes, vaccine-heavy political episodes, debunking shows and sponsor-heavy general shows. The goals were cost per window, what the model extracts, and how the keyword scan's topic hits compare with the model's labels on the same episodes.

14. Future directions

For the LLM labeling run

  • Stratify, do not sample uniformly. Health sentences are 1.6% of the corpus and narrative sentences 0.02%. A uniform window sample will spend most of its budget on sports and true crime. Use the scan as a sampling frame: over-sample windows containing narrative hits, distrust-plus-topic co-occurrence, ad windows around health products, and the 20 densest shows, while keeping a small uniform slice for recall estimation.
  • Prioritise shows by expected yield. The top 24 shows hold half of all health sentences. Labeling those first produces the most claims per GPU-hour and covers both the wellness cluster and the political cluster.
  • Use the scan as a recall check. Once labels exist for a stratum, compare the LLM's topic labels against keyword hits to estimate keyword recall per topic and LLM recall on keyword-positive windows (the supplementary keyword-retrieved audit sample the method document already calls for).
  • Adjust the evidence and certainty axes for what the model will actually see. The close reading found that wellness claims are legitimised by mechanism recital far more than by citation, that "studies show" and a named source differ seven to one in frequency, that named sources are often adversarial (Ancel Keys), and that the strongest certainty signal is presupposition ("what is your recommendation for heavy-metal detox?") rather than a marker word. A mechanistic-plausibility value on the evidence axis, a split between vague and named citation, and a "presupposed" certainty value would make those axes discriminate within the wellness genre.
  • Run ad-segment detection before evidence coding. Sponsor copy and clinical explanation are linguistically identical ("clinically proven wavelengths"); without an ad flag, the evidence axis will score advertising as evidence-bearing. Verbatim cross-episode repetition is a cheap, high-precision ad signal (section 8).
  • Verify the news narratives first. Tylenol-autism, leucovorin, hepatitis B birth dose, hantavirus, fluoride-IQ and vaccine-schedule claims have clear evidence corpora and a clear time structure, so they are the best first test of the verification stage.

Cheap extensions of the scan

  • Windowed co-occurrence. Re-run framing and certainty at the ±4 sentence level for all topics; the ad analysis shows it matters.
  • Lexicon iteration. Drop or phrase-qualify the terms the audit identified as false-positive sources, and add the narratives discovered in reading (the audit notes list several).
  • Embedding retrieval. A sentence-embedding index over the 8.4 million matched sentences would allow narrative discovery by nearest-neighbour rather than by regex, and would catch paraphrases. It needs a GPU model that is not currently installed; the shared 4070 Ti has room for a small encoder.
  • Diarisation on the dense shows. Speaker turns for the top 50 health shows would unlock the host-versus-guest question the pitch raises, at modest cost.
  • Cross-show diffusion at scale. The first-mention analysis generalises: for every bursty term, order shows by first mention and test whether the ordering is stable across events (news shows lead, political shows amplify, wellness shows lag).
  • Scripted-read detection. Exact-duplicate sentences across episodes identify sponsor reads without a marker list; the products analysis found repetition rates that could seed a classifier.

15. Methods and files

Scanner. analysis/fast/scan.py decompresses each transcript, strips speaker prefixes, splits segments into sentences on terminal punctuation, and matches the lexicon. Literal phrases (about 2,700) are compiled into a single trie-shaped regular expression with word boundaries; pattern terms (about 300, mostly narratives) are regular expressions applied only to sentences that already matched a literal, which keeps the pass fast. Output is three parquet tables: per-episode sentence and word counts, per-episode counts by term, and every matched sentence with its labels and position. The full run over 132,412 transcripts took 188 seconds on 28 cores with zero errors.

Lexicon. analysis/fast/lexicon.json, built from the two canonical tables in topics.md using the same slugified label keys as the pipeline, plus narratives, products, commercial, distrust, correction and certainty lists. Design notes are in lexicon_notes.md. Ambiguous single words were removed before the run (for example "who", "jab", "gear", "stack", "protocol", "mandate").

Pilot. analysis/topic-labeling-deepseek.toml and the deepseek provider block in analysis/usage-limits.toml configure the hosted run; analysis/fast/deepseek_pilot_analysis.py reads the run's window labels and writes deepseek_pilot.json and deepseek_pilot_notes.md; the sampled episodes are in deepseek_pilot_sample.csv. bundles.py computes the co-occurrence and repetition tables; lexicon_v2.json is the pruned lexicon used for the sensitivity rescan.

Analysis. analysis/fast/analyze.py joins the scan output to the database (show, genre, publication date), computes the tables in this report and writes analysis/output/fast-analysis/report_data.json. corpus_profile.py characterises the corpus from the database alone. products_ads.py does the sponsorship analysis. sample_audit.py draws the blinded audit sample. build_report.py assembles this page. Genre grouping is a hand mapping from Apple categories, recorded in corpus_profile.json.

Bursts use the median and median absolute deviation of each label's monthly rate across the eleven live-window months, flagging months above 3 robust standard deviations with at least 10 raw sentences. Diffusion takes the first publication date per show on which a narrative pattern matched, from August 2025 onwards.
